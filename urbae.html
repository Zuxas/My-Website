Reps Over
Theory

"I have to know how to approach every possible matchup. That takes reps, not reading." — Garrison

Garrison — Urbae — found control the moment Return to Ravnica hit the table. Supreme Verdict resetting the board, Sphinx's Revelation refilling his hand at end-of-turn — the whole archetype clicked, and it never let go. He grew up in southern Oregon, in a local scene flooded with other control players, and the shop turned into a sub-game of who could build and pilot the cleanest blue-white pile. Iteration on iteration. Mirror after mirror. The reps stacked up at a rate most players never log.

The polish came from study, too. Garrison learned the deeper layers of the archetype watching Wafo-Tapa grind MTGO challenges on YouTube — the patron saint of measured, technically perfect control play. The lessons stuck: don't lose to your own sequencing, don't tap out unless you're closing the door, every land drop matters in the end-game. Years of reps without results. Then the 2024/2025 RCQ season hit, the work compounded, and Garrison qualified for his first Regional Championship — and made Day 2.

The Philosophy

  • RepsTheory has a ceiling. Reps don't. To enter an event with confidence he has to feel how a matchup plays — what hands they keep, what mulligans they take, where the pivot points are, when the trap turns appear. That kind of knowledge can't be downloaded; it has to be grown.
  • OutplayLower-power formats are the home. When the cards aren't doing the heavy lifting, sequencing and strategy do. He doesn't want to win because his deck is two power levels above his opponent's. He wants to win because he saw the line two turns earlier than they did.
  • Best DeckNo pet-deck loyalty. The best deck of the format is always the right call. If that means learning the mirror cold, he learns the mirror cold. There's no archetype romance — only edge.

The Approach

"I need to harness the power of outplaying to gain edges." — On lower-power formats

Control as a playstyle isn't passive. It's a series of active decisions to not act yet. Every turn he doesn't tap mana is a turn he's preserving optionality — and every one of those non-decisions is the most important card in his hand. The seven modes — psychological, analytical, adaptive, reflexive, reactive, patient, pensive — all route through the same through-line.
