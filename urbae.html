Background

When did control click?
The moment Return to Ravnica hit the table. Supreme Verdict resetting the board, Sphinx's Revelation refilling his hand at end-of-turn — the whole archetype just locked in, and it never let go. He grew up in southern Oregon in a local scene flooded with other control players, and the shop turned into a sub-game of who could build and pilot the cleanest blue-white pile. Mirror after mirror. The reps stacked up at a rate most players never log.
Who shaped your play?
Wafo-Tapa — via MTGO challenges on YouTube. The patron saint of measured, technically perfect control play. The lessons stuck: don't lose to your own sequencing, don't tap out unless you're closing the door, every land drop matters in the end-game. Years of reps without results, and then the 24/25 RCQ season hit, the work compounded, and Garrison qualified for his first Regional Championship and made Day 2.

Identity

Garrison is a control player at heart, but his strongest results have all come from combo-control hybrids — decks that fuse the inevitability of a control shell with a closer that ends games on his terms. Underworld Breach, Stock Up, Consult the Star Charts, Wandering Emperor, Jace's mill ultimate. The deck doesn't need to win the long game when it can win the right one.

His philosophy on archetype selection is open and ruthless: the best deck of the format is always the right call. If that means learning the mirror cold, he learns the mirror cold. There's no pet-deck loyalty in his approach — only edge.

"I need to harness the power of outplaying to gain edges." — Garrison, on lower-power formats

Approach

How do you prep for an event?
Reps over theory. Theory has a ceiling. Reps don't. To enter an event with confidence, he has to feel how a matchup plays — what hands they keep, what mulligans they take, where the pivot points are, when the trap turns appear. That kind of knowledge can't be downloaded; it has to be grown. While other players are reading sideboard guides, Garrison is on his third rep of the same matchup, finding the lines the guide never mentioned.
Why lower-power formats?
When the cards aren't doing the heavy lifting for you, sequencing and strategy do — and that's where his edge lives. He doesn't want to win because his deck is two power levels above his opponent's. He wants to win because he saw the line two turns earlier than they did.
What does control mean to you?
Not passive. A series of active decisions to not act yet. Every turn he doesn't tap mana is a turn he's preserving optionality — and every one of those non-decisions is the most important card in his hand.

Playstyle Modes

Psychological
Reading opponent decisions — what they kept, what they fetched, what they're representing.
Analytical
Evaluating board states with cold, mathematical accuracy. No vibes-based blocking.
Adaptive
Game plan shifts mid-match. The deck he sideboards into is rarely the one he sleeved up.
Reflexive
Trigger and timing windows. The plays that disappear if you stop to think about them.
Reactive
Archetype and temperament. He plays the deck and the personality the matchup demands.
Patient
When the right play is to do nothing, he does nothing. Most players can't.
Pensive
When the right play is to think longer, he thinks longer. The clock is a resource, not a master.
The Through-Line
Outplaying. Not out-carding. Not out-rolling. Outplaying.

2026 Season

Goal for the season?
Whatever the best deck of the format is — sleeve it up, learn it cold, run it back. The reps are non-negotiable. The format is whatever the data says it should be. The edge is in the decisions everyone else is too tired to make on turn 14.
