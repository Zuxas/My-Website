Team Resolve · Process

deck building

This isn't a generic guide to what a mana curve is. This is how Team Resolve actually builds and locks a 75 for competitive play — the process behind 5 RC qualifications across formats.

"Every card in your deck needs to answer one question: what does this do for my game plan in the matchups I expect to face?"

the principles we actually use

Principle 01
Know Your Role
Before you build a deck, know whether it's the beatdown or the control in each expected matchup. Build around the role, not just the card power level.
Principle 02
Test the Field
Run gauntlets against the top 6–8 expected matchups. Win rates against the actual field matter. Win rates against your favorite matchup don't.
Principle 03
Lock the 75
Two undecided sideboard slots the morning of an event is not a 75. Every slot needs a specific reason against a specific threat in the expected field.
Principle 04
Redundancy Wins
Mistcaller + Consign to Memory + Solitude overwhelms their answers. Mulliganing to one perfect card loses to anyone who has more than one answer.
Principle 05
Mana Base Is Load-Bearing
Bad mana loses games the deck would otherwise win. Every non-basic should be justified against the expected field. Know which cost — tempo or life — matters more per matchup.
Principle 06
Familiarity Beats Optimization
A slightly suboptimal list played at 95% beats an optimal list played at 70%. Pick a deck and get reps. The decisions that matter in a long tournament only come from repetition.

the process in detail

1. Mana Curve — What It Actually Means

The mana curve isn't a target to hit — it's a function of your strategy. The UW Blink shell illustrates this well: it looks two-drop heavy but it's not designed to curve out. It's designed to use Ephemerate to get multiple uses out of two-drops on the same turn. Density of action at two mana matters more than "the curve."

1–2 Mana
The Foundation
These set up everything else. In aggro they're your clock. In tempo they're your disruption and value generators. Aim for 8–12 cards you're happy to play on turns one and two.
3–4 Mana
The Engine
Where midrange and control come online. Cards here should protect your early threats, generate significant value, or both. Don't fill this slot with cards that don't pull double duty.
5+ Mana
Close the Game
Limit severely unless your deck reliably reaches them. A 5+ mana card that doesn't immediately win the game is a liability. Teferi, Hero of Dominaria earns his slot — most cards don't.
2. Mana Base — The Part Most Players Get Wrong

The single most common mistake at the RCQ level is a mana base optimized for the best-case scenario rather than the matchup-dependent scenario.

How we evaluate each land slot

  • Fetchlands: Always correct to run the maximum in two-color or more. Deck thinning is real across long tournaments.
  • Shocklands: Know which matchups punish you for paying two life and when to take the free turn instead.
  • Fastlands / Checklands: Evaluate how often they come in untapped based on your curve — a fastland in a five-drop-heavy deck is a tapped land most of the time.
  • Utility lands: Every utility land is a spell that doesn't count toward your land count. Flagstones of Trokair is correct in the Phantom shell specifically. Most utility lands are worse than they look.

Target land counts

  • Aggro: 20–22 lands. Hit two mana reliably, rarely need more than three.
  • Midrange / Tempo: 22–24 lands. Hit three consistently, occasionally four.
  • Control: 24–26 lands. Flood is survivable. Screw is often fatal.
3. The Sideboard — 15 Cards, Not an Afterthought
"A good sideboard makes your bad matchups beatable. A great sideboard makes them favorable. Never bring in more than 7 cards — over-boarding dilutes your strategy."

How we build a sideboard

  • Identify your three worst matchups in the expected field. Based on gauntlet data, not theory. These get addressed first.
  • Assign a purpose to every card. If a card is only good in one matchup, it needs to be a game-ending card to justify the slot.
  • Plan your sideboard games before the event. For every major matchup, know what comes in and what comes out. This should be automatic, not made under tournament stress.
  • Verify Needle targets before registering. A wrong Pithing Needle name is a wasted slot for the entire event.

Current sideboard cards we're running or evaluating

  • Consign to Memory — hard answer to Reanimator and Belcher lines. 3 copies if those matchups are in the expected field.
  • Sanctifier en-Vec — hard lock vs. black recursion and red hate. High priority against the current field.
  • Stony Silence — beats Affinity and artifact-based combo cleanly. Slam on curve, never hold.
  • Teferi, Time Raveler — improves combo matchups, protects your sorceries. Slam early.
  • Wrath of the Skies — tempo card, not a win condition. Buy two rebuild turns, not value.
4. Card Selection — How We Evaluate Individual Cards

The questions we ask

  • What does this do in my worst matchups? This is where a card earns or loses its slot. Does it give you an angle you don't otherwise have?
  • What does this do in the mirror? Mirror matches are often decided by sideboard construction. A card that's dominant in the mirror is worth considering if the mirror is common.
  • What do I cut for this? Adding a card always means cutting one. The question isn't whether the new card is good — it's whether it's better than the card it's replacing in the contexts that matter.

Card advantage isn't just drawing cards. It's generating more resources per unit of mana than your opponent. Ephemerate is card advantage. Solitude is card advantage. Recognizing this in non-obvious places separates players who understand the format from players who just know the popular decks.

5. Consistency — The Underrated Variable

The most consistent deck beats the most powerful deck over a large enough sample. At RCQ volume — I've played 40+ RCQs across the last two seasons — consistency compounds. A deck that goes 5–1 every event beats a deck that goes 6–0 sometimes and 3–3 others.

How we maximize consistency

  • Run 4-ofs on your most important cards. If you need to see Ephemerate, run 4. Don't dilute key cards with cute one-ofs unless the one-of specifically answers a problem no other card addresses.
  • Aggressively mulligan. A bad hand is a game you're playing at a disadvantage from turn one. Six with the right cards beats seven with the wrong ones.
  • Know your keepable hands before the event. The UW Blink guide defines snap keeps and snap mulligans explicitly. That decision should be automatic at the table — not made fresh under pressure.
