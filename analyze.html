analyzing deck lists

Looking at a top 8 decklist and copying it is not analysis. Analysis is understanding why each card is there, what the flex slots tell you about the expected field, and what the sideboard reveals about the pilot's matchup assumptions.

"A decklist is a hypothesis. The pilot is arguing these 75 cards are the best answer to the expected field at that event. Your job is to figure out what they were expecting — and whether they were right."

the analysis framework

Step 01
Identify the Game Plan
What is this deck trying to do? Not the archetype label — the specific sequence of plays that wins the game. "Reanimate Atraxa" is a game plan. "Play midrange" is not.
Step 02
Find the Core Cards
Which cards are 4-ofs? These define what the deck cannot function without. Attack the thing it needs — don't waste interaction on the redundant parts.
Step 03
Read the Flex Slots
The 1-ofs and 2-ofs in the main deck are signal. They reveal what the pilot was hedging against in the expected field.
Step 04
Decode the Sideboard
The sideboard tells you what the deck is afraid of. The hate pieces, quantities, and what's absent all carry information about expected matchups.
Step 05
Map the Matchups
Which matchups does this list beat cleanly? Which is it hoping to dodge? A deck 70/30 in six matchups but losing badly to two is a liability in a diverse RC field.
Step 06
Evaluate Tournament Context
A deck winning a 40-player RCQ is not the same data point as winning a 300-player RC. Context determines how much weight to give the result.

each step in depth

1. Identify the Game Plan

Before looking at individual card choices, establish what the deck is trying to accomplish. Not the archetype — the specific win condition and the sequence of plays that gets there.

Boros Energy's game plan: apply pressure from turn one with Ragavan or Guide of Souls, develop the energy/lifegain synergy through Ocelot Pride, and close with Ajani's pump before the opponent stabilizes. Every card in the 75 should execute that plan or protect it from being disrupted. If you can't articulate the game plan in two sentences, you don't understand the deck well enough to evaluate the card choices correctly.

2. Find the Core Cards

Core cards are the 4-ofs. Identify them and ask: what does the deck do when it doesn't draw this card? If the answer is "very little," that tells you how to beat the deck. Attack the thing the deck needs.

In the blink shell, Ephemerate is the core card. The deck functions without any individual creature but not at full power without Ephemerate. That's why the correct plan against blink always involves answering threats at instant speed before Ephemerate resolves — not after.

In Prowess, both DRC and Swiftspear are core. The deck has redundancy at the threat level — which is why effective hate targets the spells, not the creatures. Chalice of the Void at one counters Lightning Bolt, Lava Dart, and Mutagenic Growth simultaneously. Dress Down removes DRC's delirium and Cori-Steel Cutter's Flurry ability in one card. Sanctifier en-Vec has protection from red — your entire burn suite deals zero damage to it. The correct hate isn't answering individual threats; it's collapsing the engine those threats depend on.

3. Read the Flex Slots

The most information-dense part of any decklist is the flex slots. A singleton or two-of in the main is almost always a meta call — the pilot expected something specific and included that card as an answer.

  • One copy of a removal spell handling a specific threat — tells you what the pilot was afraid of in the expected field.
  • A utility land in the 23rd slot — tells you about the mana count and what the deck is willing to trade for the utility effect.
  • An off-archetype card providing a specific angle — tells you the pilot expected that matchup to be common.

A singleton Consign to Memory in the main of a blink list tells us the pilot expected combo to show up. That's a piece of intel about the expected field at that specific event.

4. Decode the Sideboard

The sideboard is the most honest part of a decklist. Hard choices were made about which matchups to shore up and which to leave vulnerable. Reading those choices tells you what was expected.

What to look for

  • Volume of graveyard hate — how concerned is the pilot about Reanimator or Phoenix variants?
  • Combo disruption quantities — Consign, Orim's Chant, Force of Negation counts indicate how much the pilot respects combo in the expected field.
  • Sweeper count — more sweepers means expecting aggressive fields.
  • What's absent is as important as what's present. No graveyard hate in a field with Reanimator at 17% is a mistake or a deliberate gamble. Figure out which.

3–4 copies of a sideboard card means the pilot considers that matchup critical. 1 copy means access without dedicating slots. 0 means they either didn't expect the matchup or have a different plan.

5. Map the Matchups

Once you understand the list, map out which matchups it's built to beat and which it's hoping to dodge. Every deck in Modern has bad matchups. The question is whether those bad matchups overlap with the expected field at your event.

A deck that's 70/30 against six of the top eight matchups but loses badly to the other two is still strong if those two don't show up at your RCQ. It's a liability at an RC where the field is diverse and prepared.

At Team Resolve we track win/loss records by matchup across gauntlet sessions and tournament results. After enough data, the noise filters out and you see whether the deck holds up against the full field or just the favorable half.

6. Evaluate Tournament Context

Not all results are equal. Before drawing conclusions from a top 8, evaluate:

  • Event size: A 40-player RCQ has a very different field than a 500-player open.
  • Format: A result at a Modern RCQ doesn't necessarily transfer to RC Modern, where the field is more prepared.
  • Pilot: A world-class pilot top-8ing with a fringe deck is signal. A solid local player with a tier-one deck in a field with no hate is much weaker signal.
  • Field composition: A deck that won by going 6-0 against aggro in a field that was 70% aggro is not positioned well for a diverse RC field.

a worked example — evaluating boros energy

When we evaluated whether to run back Boros Energy for this Modern season, we went through the full framework:

  • Game plan: Clear. Pressure turn one, advantage engine through Guide + Ocelot, close with Ajani.
  • Core cards: Ragavan, Guide, Ocelot, Ajani. All 4-ofs. Non-negotiable.
  • Flex slots: One or two copies of Searing Blood vs Wild Slash tell us how the pilot weighted creature-heavy vs burn-heavy matchups.
  • Sideboard read: Sanctifier en-Vec in opponent lists is now universal. The field knows what the deck does and is ready for it.
  • Matchup map: Still beats unfocused decks cleanly. Struggles against prepared opponents with clean hate — which is exactly what an RC field looks like.
  • Context: Last season it was underplayed and underrespected. This season it's the known quantity. Margins are tighter.

That analysis is why we're investing testing time in the blink shell rather than just running the deck back — the conditions that made it correct last season have partially changed.
